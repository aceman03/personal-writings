# Writing Rules

> Author's working rules for every book in the library. Not linked from the website.

## 1. Published chapters are set in stone

Once a new chapter is started, every chapter before it is **locked**.

- Before writing a new chapter, reread **every** earlier chapter and build on exactly what they say.
- If something earlier turns out to be inconsistent, awkward or wrong, **do not edit the earlier chapter.**
  Resolve it inside the new chapter instead: explain it, reframe it, or have a character notice it.
- Fixes, rereads and revisions apply **only to the chapter currently being written.**
- Site material that describes earlier chapters (character pages, map notes, the codex) may still be
  updated for new information, but must never contradict what a locked chapter says.

## 1a. Review every previous chapter before starting the next

Added at the author's request on 2026-11-10. **Before any new chapter is written:**

1. **Reread every previous chapter in full**, from Chapter One onward, not just the last one.
2. **Check continuity and consistency** across all of them: timeline and day count, head counts and names,
   geography and distances, the rules of magic and blessings, what each character knows and when,
   character voices, and setups that still need paying off.
3. **Check the cross-chapter repetition** of phrases and images (see Prose style).
4. **Check the site material** (character pages, map, codex, outline) against the chapters.
5. **Report the findings** before writing. Problems in the most recent chapter are fixed there, since it is
   still open. Problems in locked chapters are resolved inside the new chapter (Rule 1), unless the author
   explicitly unlocks them.
6. **Only then lock the previous chapter and begin the new one**, building on exactly what is on the page.

## 2. Chapter length

15–20 printed pages (about 4,500–6,000 words). Occasionally as short as 5 pages or as long as 50.

## 3. Every chapter ends on a cliffhanger

Tied to a major plot point, leading into the next chapter.

## 4. Before publishing a new chapter

- The full review in Rule 1a must already have been done before writing began.

- Reread the new chapter for logic, timeline and continuity against every locked chapter.
- Check every narration cue: speakers have voice profiles, sound effects and ambience exist.
- New characters get a spoiler-free page (only what is known at their first appearance), a portrait and a voice.
- New places go on the map.
- Mark the chapter as written in the book outline.

## 5. Prose style

Habits to keep rare (a few deliberate uses per chapter at most):

- **"It wasn't X. It was Y."** and its cousins ("Not X, but Y"). Save it for a real reveal.
- **"for a long moment" / "for a long time."** Show the length instead: what happens during it.
- **"very."** Find the stronger word.
- **"quietly" / "softly" dialogue tags.** The mood markup already tells the narrator; let the line carry it.
- **Lists of three, "the way you…" similes, and "It was a…" openers.** Vary sentence shape and rhythm.
- **Watch for repeats across chapters, not just within one.** Before publishing, scan the whole book for
  phrases and images reused between chapters ("every one of them", "had never once", tongue-in-a-gap and
  hound similes). Keep a repeat only when it is a deliberate refrain or callback.

## 6. Vocabulary

Rich, varied and precise, but never showy. The prose should flow, not sound like a thesaurus.

- **Precise verbs over generic ones.** Avoid leaning on *came, went, stood, looked, walked, felt, made*.
  Reach for the verb that carries the motion or the feeling: *spilled, burst, trudged, lunged, seeped,
  filed in, ground on, eyed, regarded, crested, plunged, sank.*
- **No word repeated close together** unless the repetition is deliberate (rhythm, echo, emphasis).
  Before publishing, scan for content words used twice within about 25 words, and generic verbs
  used more than a handful of times per chapter.
- **Keep plain words where plainness is the point.** "A soldier went down." "A mule went lame."
- **"Said" stays.** It is the invisible dialogue tag; don't replace it with *exclaimed, opined, retorted*.
- **Match the character.** In close point of view, use words the character would think in: Hask thinks in
  plain soldier's words, Ilsabet in technical ones, Corvel like a twelve-year-old.
- **Vary sentence shape.** Mix long, flowing sentences with short ones; avoid strings of sentences that
  all start the same way.

## 8. Moral gray

The Hollow Choir and its people should be genuinely tempting, and the Bright Court's institutions
genuinely flawed. Let readers understand why someone would take a dark blessing. Keep the gray small
and specific: a cure that works, a temple that charged for healing, a war god who fed starving children.
